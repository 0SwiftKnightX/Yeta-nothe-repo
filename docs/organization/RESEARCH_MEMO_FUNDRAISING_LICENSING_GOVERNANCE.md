## Executive summary

The current evidence supports continuing to prepare a transparent early-stage project, but not presenting the initiative as a registered charity, tax-exempt organization, established company, or funded platform. A GoFundMe personal/organization fundraiser may be a candidate for personal or business-purpose fundraising, but the recipient, use of funds, fees, taxes, and current platform requirements must be finalized before launch. A nonprofit fundraiser is a different route and requires an eligible nonprofit/charity status. The historical custom-license idea must be separated from the open-source software licensing strategy: an open-source license cannot prohibit redistribution, forks, or derivative works in ways that conflict with the Open Source Definition.

## 1. GoFundMe: current platform rules reviewed

Primary sources:
- [GoFundMe Terms of Service](https://www.gofundme.com/c/terms) — effective date shown on the page: June 23, 2026.
- [Choosing a fundraiser type](https://support.gofundme.com/hc/en-us/articles/203603984-Choosing-a-fundraiser-type) — page updated August 28, 2026.
- [Guidelines for a trustworthy GoFundMe](https://support.gofundme.com/hc/en-us/articles/115015836967-Guidelines-for-a-trustworthy-GoFundMe) — page updated August 25, 2026.

Findings:
1. GoFundMe distinguishes personal/organization fundraisers from nonprofit fundraisers. The help page lists businesses among causes that can use the personal/organization route; eligible nonprofit fundraisers have separate eligibility and transfer rules.
2. The terms place responsibility on organizers to describe the use of funds clearly and accurately, use funds for the stated purpose, follow applicable laws, and handle applicable taxes. GoFundMe does not guarantee fundraiser success or tax deductibility.
3. GoFundMe's current terms say it does not charge a platform fee to start or maintain a fundraiser, but transaction/payment-processing fees apply. The exact current rate and net payout must be confirmed in the fundraiser flow before setting a target.
4. The help page says personal/organization fundraiser donations are typically personal gifts and are not guaranteed to be tax-deductible. Do not call donations tax-deductible unless the actual recipient and legal/tax facts support that statement.
5. The trustworthy-fundraiser guidance says the story should explain what funds pay for, identify who is responsible for those expenses, and explain how money will reach the intended beneficiary if the organizer is raising funds on someone else's behalf.
6. The terms prohibit offering goods or services in exchange for donations. Do not promise equity, repayment, guaranteed returns, or reward-like exchanges without checking the actual terms and applicable law.

## 2. Ohio legal and charitable-registration considerations

Primary sources:
- [Ohio Attorney General — Starting a Charity in Ohio](https://charitable.ohioago.gov/Charity-Registration/Starting-a-Charity-in-Ohio)
- [Ohio Revised Code §1716.02](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-1716.02)
- [Ohio Attorney General — Online Charitable Registration Information Sheet](https://charitable.ohioago.gov/Charity-Registration/Publications/Online-Charitable-Registration-Information-Sheet)

Findings:
1. The Ohio Attorney General explains that the first steps depend on the intended legal form; a nonprofit corporation generally files articles with the Ohio Secretary of State, while other forms may have different requirements.
2. The Ohio Attorney General states that state incorporation is separate from federal tax exemption: only the IRS determines federal tax-exempt status, and a charity is not automatically exempt just because it exists as a nonprofit.
3. Ohio law requires covered charitable organizations soliciting contributions in Ohio to register before soliciting and annually thereafter, subject to statutory exemptions. Applicability depends on the actual entity, activity, and exemptions.
4. Registered organizations can have continuing reporting and document-maintenance obligations. These are ongoing responsibilities, not one-time formation steps.

## 3. Open-source licensing and the historical custom-license concept

Primary sources:
- [Open Source Initiative — The Open Source Definition](https://opensource.org/osd)
- [Open Source Initiative — Frequently Asked Questions](https://opensource.org/faq)
- [Open Source Initiative — License list](https://opensource.org/licenses)
- [SPDX License List](https://spdx.org/licenses/) — the page showed version 3.29.0, dated September 16, 2026, when reviewed.

Findings:
1. The Open Source Definition requires, among other criteria, free redistribution and permission to create and distribute derived works. It does not permit discrimination against persons, groups, or fields of endeavor.
2. OSI's FAQ explains that open-source software can be used commercially and that a license cannot forbid use by disfavored people or fields. It also explains that open-source licensing does not automatically grant permission to use the project's trademarks.
3. A restriction designed to prevent all forks, cloning, redistribution, or derivative works would conflict with core open-source freedoms. If that restriction is essential, the asset may need a different source-available/proprietary license rather than being called open source.
4. The SPDX License List provides standardized identifiers and canonical license references. Use a recognized identifier when the actual license has been identified; do not invent an SPDX ID or infer the license solely from a repository's default label.

## 4. Governance decision framework

No single legal structure is selected. Compare at least:
- Founder-controlled for-profit company.
- Nonprofit corporation seeking tax-exempt status if eligible and appropriate.
- Member-governed organization or cooperative form, where legally available and suitable.
- Fiscal sponsorship through an existing eligible nonprofit, subject to a written agreement.
- A hybrid arrangement only if the additional legal/accounting complexity is justified.

Evaluate each option on: asset ownership, founder/operator control, succession, contributor rights, ability to pay the founder, donation/grant eligibility, reporting duties, liability, tax treatment, fundraising restrictions, and long-term community governance. The phrase “the organization owns itself” must be translated into specific legal rights and decision-making rules.
